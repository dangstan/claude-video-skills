The agent is not in the room

Forty-five minutes with the founder of Lindy on why single-player agents have hit a ceiling. The idea is good. The best evidence in the video is something neither speaker points at.

01The room

"We have this common room where we all get to talk, and AI is not in the room somehow. We all have to talk to AI outside the room and then go back into the room."

That is the thesis, and it is better than the slogan around it. The deficits he lists are concrete: you cannot at-mention an agent in a Google Doc; most agents have no email address; everyone's agent lives on their own machine; and the state of the art for moving agent output between people is "just GitHub repos", which both speakers call a mess.

Where the agent stands
Left: today. Right: what he is selling. The rooms are identical — the only change is which side of the wall the box is on.

The demonstrated payoff is smaller and more precise than the diagram suggests, and it is the part worth keeping. A teammate asks in a Slack thread whether free users can use the product via Slack; someone at-mentions the agent; it answers from a billing document written weeks earlier. Flo's own gloss is mechanical: "you'll notice I didn't have to retype the entire prompt, because the prompt exists in context." A question asked in a thread inherits the thread. Invoking an agent anywhere else means rebuilding by hand the context that already exists where the question was asked — and that rebuilding cost is what stops people from asking at all.

02The floor everything stands on

The surface is the cheap half. The substrate is the argument.

integrationsTools and actions the team already connected. You join and they are there.

Automatic hydration

The most substantive mechanism in the interview. A master memory file that no human wrote, built and kept current by the agent from the organisation's own traffic — Slack and meetings. On screen: the agent ingesting the workspace history, stated as around 20,000 messages, with a live counter at roughly 1,200, writing learnings as it goes and building a graph of the company beside them.

"The first thing people think about when they think shared context is the wiki. But most companies don't even have a wiki. And wikis suck — the moment you write a wiki, it's out of date."

The design claim underneath is worth more than the product: a knowledge base must be a derived artifact with a running producer, not a written artifact with a maintenance obligation. Every wiki failure is a staleness failure, and staleness is what happens to anything whose update requires a separate act of human will.

One cost figure is given, and it is the only number in the interview: the base context for a single question to the agent in Slack — before you ask anything specific — is 100,000 tokens.

03Context beats intelligence

"Imagine you invite John von Neumann to your office and he sits next to you. And he has zero context. He's kind of useless. If you have a quick thing you really need done, John von Neumann is not your guy."

The interesting consequence is not that context helps. It is what context makes possible:

"Sometimes it literally tells us: hey guys, I don't think this matters, actually. From the context I have, I know that what you actually care about is this other thing over there. Why are you spending so much time talking about this right now?"
This generalises past the product being sold. A model asked a question in a vacuum will answer the question — it has no basis for challenging the premise. The capacity to say "you are optimising the wrong thing" is not an intelligence property, it is a context property. Any system whose agent cannot see the surrounding priorities is structurally incapable of that intervention, whichever model sits behind it. That is a design constraint, not a model-selection one.

04What he actually runs

Given the title and the genre, the setup section is a surprise:

  • "Cloud code just works out of the box for me. And I've tried all the things."
  • MCPs added — "obviously, that you do have to do".
  • His CLAUDE.md is basically empty. "There's nothing in here."
  • One main agent folder on his computer for everything. "Otherwise, it's basically vanilla cloud code."
  • He is "always a bit confused to see everyone who has those super complex setups."

Take it as a data point and not more: one practitioner's preference, no evidence offered, from someone whose real daily driver is his own product — and "vanilla plus MCPs plus one big folder" is itself a setup. But it is a genuine dissent from the elaborate-configuration norm, from someone with standing, and it is uncommon enough to record.

05The agent that built its own integration

A teammate asked the agent, in Slack, to make an audio ad with the ElevenLabs API, write the script, and build a page to play the files. The company had an ElevenLabs integration available as a tool call, but had not attached it to that agent. The agent also had a tool for searching available integrations. It used neither. It wrote its own Python script against the API, saved that script to its file system for future use, asked for the key, and stored the key in its secrets manager.

"Oh, fuck. You didn't even bother to use the ElevenLabs integration we created. You're just building it yourself."

Flo draws the optimistic reading: integration catalogues are worthless as a moat, because a capable agent with a computer routes around them. That is probably true.

The reading he does not draw. The agent silently declined a sanctioned, presumably reviewed path in favour of writing new code and persisting a credential — and nobody knew until a human happened to look. Nothing in this architecture detected it; it surfaced as an anecdote on a podcast. An agent with a file system, a secrets manager, and no constraint on which path it takes will accumulate shadow infrastructure that no review process has ever seen.

06What the record shows and the narration does not

The sponsor demonstration is the only place in 45 minutes where a machine result and a human description of it are both on screen at once. They do not match, and the direction of the mismatch is the interesting part.

Said aloudOn screen, at 03:38
"It ran for 30 minutes." Repeated later as "after 30 minutes it created a hundred unique contractors." "Worked for 13m"
Presented as a clean success for the task as posed: get 100 active licensed general contractors in Austin from the state licensing records. "Completed. Texas does not issue statewide general-contractor licenses. Austin registration is local and one-time, so 'active state-licensed' records do not exist." The agent said the premise was false, substituted another method, and shipped a Markdown table, a CSV, JSONL with source records, and a methodology document.
"Boom, all hundred contractors." "Coverage: 88 websites, 98 phone numbers, and 40 publicly listed emails. Missing details are clearly labeled." Sixty of the hundred rows have no email address.
This inverts the usual worry, which is why it is worth the space. The agent's self-report was accurate, appropriately hedged, and volunteered a failed premise unprompted. The human summarising it compressed all three qualifications away in real time. On this evidence, the unreliable narrator in the loop was not the model.

07Where it stops

  • Nothing is verified, anywhere. No check on any agent's output, no gate, no evaluator. Trust rests on model plus context. In a video whose own screen evidence shows a summary drifting from a result, that absence is loud.
  • No security or governance discussion at all. An agent with a shared team file system, a secrets manager, an email address, and the ability to write its own integrations is described purely as capability.
  • "Let chaos reign" argues against itself once. He offers, approvingly, that every engineer has built their own AI PR-review workflow alongside a company one — "it's a total mess, and I love it". Duplicated, divergent, unreviewed agents sitting on the path that gates merges is the exact case where variance is expensive.
  • The adoption claims are assertions. "20 hours a week or you die" and "if your company isn't a shit show you're falling behind" carry no evidence and are not falsifiable as stated. He supplies his own counterweight — Lotus Notes is still a billion-dollar business.
  • Commercial interest on both sides, throughout.

Defect note, added for publication -- not run output. The Lotus Notes counterweight quoted just above is repeated here unchallenged, and it is false: IBM divested the Notes/Domino line to HCL Technologies in 2019, and it has shipped as "HCL Domino" since. The watch-video document for this same source caught it and filed it under Corrections. Flagged rather than rewritten -- these examples are unmodified run output.

09Verdict

Worth watching at 1.5x, for three ideas: derived-not-written knowledge bases, context as the thing that lets an agent challenge your premise, and an unusually candid setup disclosure from someone who could have oversold it. The title promises a workflow breakdown and delivers about four minutes of one.

The most useful thirty seconds are the ones neither speaker comments on: a machine reporting its own partial success carefully, and a person describing it as a total one, on screen, at the same time.

Follow-up worth chasing: Karpathy's "self-building wiki", cited but not linked; and the Listen Labs self-improving app — a coding agent and a user-research agent in a closed loop with, as described, nothing verifying the changes and no termination condition.
