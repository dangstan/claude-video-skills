    What the exchange actually was

    A screening call conducted in a borrowed language

    That the English was borrowed is not an inference. It is negotiated in the first minute, in Portuguese, before any of it. The interviewer asks whether they can do the rest of the conversation in English; the candidate says yes, and that he actually prefers it, because he is more used to giving these interviews in English. The English window is a deliberate exercise for both of them, and the close is the two of them dropping the exercise once the evaluative part is over and the transactional part begins.

    The shape of the English window is monologue-and-response, lopsided first in one direction and then the other. The interviewer opens with a continuous four-minute company overview. The candidate then holds the floor for most of the next six minutes and answers five open questions at length. Across the whole English window he takes 70.3 % of the speaking time on 65.7 % of the words -- speaking for more of the clock than his word count accounts for, which is the arithmetic signature of slow, unpaused delivery rather than fast delivery.

    The call goes well. Its strongest moment is unprompted and, in a way the recording could not have anticipated, self-referential: asked how he stays current, the candidate spends five minutes describing his own multi-agent orchestration setup, and ends on a tool he built to make an assistant watch a video for him.

    you can say, transcribe the audio, read the transcription. Then, every time you feel like you want to see what is happening in the video while you are reading the transcription, extract a frame from the video for that specific moment. transcript 20:27

    That is, step for step, the pipeline that produced this page. The interviewer's reply is specific rather than a polite backchannel -- she volunteers that she has recently started building skills for the same assistant at work, and that it helps her a great deal. It is the only point in the recording where she discloses something about her own practice.

    What landed

    Three moments the transcript reads wrong

    A transcript cannot say how something was received. Each of these was resolved by cutting a nine-frame reaction sheet across twelve seconds and reading it.

    • The push-back on her own length. After a four-minute overview he says he had expected a brief one and got a lot, and thanks her for it. Read cold that is a mild criticism from a candidate to a recruiter. The frames show her moving from a neutral speaking face to a broad, open, eye-crinkling smile across the following eight seconds while he is still talking. It was received as charm.
    • The word he could not find. Asked about ideal company culture he stalls outright -- he cannot find the word in English and asks how to translate it; she answers that she does not know. The two longest pauses in the whole English window, 2.30 s and 2.01 s, sit inside this exchange. Read from the transcript alone it is the worst moment in the round. The frames show both of them smiling within four seconds and her still smiling eight seconds later. It cost him time, not rapport.
    • The salary number. Asked directly for an expectation in dollars, he names one, with a visible search and a hedge, framed as what he has been asking elsewhere. Her face across the following eight seconds is neutral: no smile, no surprise, no lean. She moves straight to the availability question. That is a genuine null, and it is reported as one -- the recording cannot say whether the number was high, low or expected.

    Method

    How the pixels were made to check the transcript

    Attribution in a two-party call is the assumption most likely to be wrong and least likely to be checked. Here the primary source is a speaker-separated caption export, which is exact where it is legible and useless where it is not.

    The independent check is Google Meet's own active-speaker indicator: the thin border the interface draws around whichever tile is speaking, measured as blue-channel excess over a four-pixel strip at the top edge of each tile, on every extracted frame. It shares nothing with the export -- one is caption text, the other is a rendered interface element.

    Across the English window the two agree on 1361 of 1488 decided seconds, 91.5 %, and their aggregate talk-share differs by 0.2 percentage points. The border is lit on both tiles in zero frames of the recording, which is the structural check that the probe reads one indicator rather than two brightness artifacts.

    The face-to-name map was bound from two disjoint sources -- the nameplate the interface renders inside each tile, and the active-speaker border lighting on a tile at times the caption panel attributes the line to that person -- and re-verified at 02:00, 10:00 and 28:20. Grid order and geometry are unchanged at all three.

    The recording's own on-screen live captions settle the export's failure directly rather than by inference: at 28:20 they read Titan., And complements. The., Make, follow. -- the same nonsense the export carries at the same timestamps, visible in the pixels.

    Two audio channels turned out to be the same signal: difference ratio 0.81 %, correlation 0.999967 at lag 0, cross-correlation peak at exactly lag 0. Same-signal branch, mono downmix, no alignment work, and no prosody claim anywhere on this page -- a single mixed track cannot support one.

    Limits

    What this page cannot tell you

    • Word counts, filler counts and exact turn text exist only for the 79.0 % of the recording the export can read. The remaining 21.0 % is fully recovered by a second decode, but not by the same instrument, so no statistic is computed across the two.
    • The package's own prescribed way of detecting a language switch -- running language identification over the transcript text -- does not work on this file. Driven in English, the model emitted English for Portuguese speech, so language identification over its output reports English everywhere. What found the switch: the export collapsing, an in-recording line thanking him for speaking English, a repetition loop, and a re-decode.
    • The verbatim tier fabricates about fourteen seconds of filler at 13:20-13:42 and loops one sentence seven times at 25:56-26:03. Both are excluded from quotation. A systematic scan finds no other repeat run of three or more segments.
    • Reaction reading is limited to one participant. The other wears glasses that catch the monitor's reflection in most frames, so no eye-direction claim is made about him; the postures cited are gross posture. Gaze cannot distinguish reading from thinking without a contrasting baseline, and none exists here.
    • Whether an answer-generating assistant was running is unmeasured. No log exists to overlap against what was said, and absence of a log is not evidence of absence. No assistant surface appears in any extracted frame, which bounds the captured region and not the machine.
