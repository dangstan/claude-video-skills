Two Caption Tracks, One Call

A tech-company screening interview ran two independent transcription systems at once -- Google Meet's live burned-in captions, and an offline whisper decode -- and neither one alone was trustworthy. Comparing them is what caught the difference between a candidate pausing and a model hallucinating.

Participants: the recruiter (the company) & the candidate Role: Lead AI Engineer Date: 2026-08-25 Duration: 31m 33s (1892.7s) Source: local recording, Google Meet
Ingest for this report: 1 fps frame extraction (1893 frames), 12 individual frames read at flagged timestamps to cross-check the burned-in Meet caption against the whisper decode; no whisper re-run (transcript reused from a prior run against the same source); no frame bursts needed. Independent pass: written without reading the prior run's own knowledge document first.

This is a first-round recruiter screening call, not a technical interview. Its content is a straightforward hiring conversation. What makes the material worth a design pass is a verification problem that the transcript itself created: the source decode contains several runs of an identical short phrase repeated far more times than a human could physically say them in the time allotted. Those are whisper hallucinations. But the video also carries Google Meet's own live captions burned into the picture, in a separate language track that itself gets confused whenever the conversation switches into Portuguese. Neither track is reliable on its own -- one hallucinates repeats, the other garbles words it cannot parse -- but where they disagree, that disagreement is diagnostic. Read the chart as a triangulation exercise. The whisper track is the one anyone would read if they only had a transcript. It is wrong twice, in a way that is easy to miss on a casual read because each wrong run still looks like plausible transcript text -- until you notice the timestamps span barely two seconds for six repeats of a four-word phrase. The Meet track is wrong differently: it does not repeat, it degrades into nonsense the moment the underlying speech leaves English, which is exactly what a live translator under load does. Where they agree, that agreement is confirmation. Where the whisper track shows something impossible (six repeats in two seconds), the Meet track resolving cleanly at that same moment is what proves the hallucination. Where the Meet track is nonsense and the whisper track is a genuine Portuguese sentence, that is the opposite kind of evidence -- the Meet track failing is consistent with real non-English speech, not proof against it.

Corrections

Three anomalies in the source transcript were checked against the burned-in caption track. Two are confirmed hallucinations and have been corrected in the accompanying transcript file; one is corroborated but not confirmed, and has been left in place with a flag rather than silently fixed or silently trusted.

Confirmed hallucination -- 5:56 (356s)

The source transcript reads "and to the next level" repeated six times inside a two-second window -- not physically sayable. The Meet caption for this exact moment shows a single continuous sentence instead:

"...and and how it can help us bring our ideas because I, I see myself as a very creative person..."

The candidate was connecting "how it can" to "help us bring our ideas," not repeating a phrase about a "next level" at all.

Confirmed hallucination -- 22:58-23:17 (1378s-1397s)

The source transcript reads the single word "Yeah." repeated eighteen times across this window. The Meet caption for the same window shows one natural sentence from the recruiter, running straight into the candidate's real answer with no repeat at all:

"...I'm happy to know that as well and. And when you think about an ideal company culture environment(s) that helps to bring out the best of you as a professional, what type of company culture comes to your mind?" -> "I think it resonates specifically with that culture because as I said, I am someone who is..."
Flagged, corroborated but unconfirmed -- 28:59-29:14 (1738s-1754s)

The source transcript reads the Portuguese hesitation filler "E..." (roughly "and...") repeated fifteen times. Unlike the two cases above, the Meet caption for this window does not resolve cleanly -- it renders as nonsense ("Namaste problem," "Palestination. Atlas, California."), which is consistent with the live auto-translator failing on genuine, sustained Portuguese speech. That corroborates a real hesitation-filled pause happening here, but it does not confirm the specific word or the exact fifteen-times count. This one was left in the transcript as-is, flagged rather than corrected -- the evidence available only bounds what happened, it does not pin it down.

Verdict

As a hiring signal, this call did its job cleanly: company context landed, role scope was clarified (including a self-correction caught on tape), logistics were confirmed, and next steps are unambiguous. As source material for this report, its real value is procedural rather than substantive -- it is a clean demonstration that a transcript should not be trusted just because it reads fluently, and that a second, independently-wrong signal (here, Google Meet's own live captions) can do more to locate the truth than either source alone. The specific correction method used here -- treat a repeated-phrase run as a red flag, then check it against whatever second signal exists in the frames -- generalizes past this one call.
